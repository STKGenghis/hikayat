# Experience and design principles

## Interaction principles

### Let the harm emerge

Do not state the lesson in the opening brief. Present signals and incentives
first. Name the relevant AI-safety concepts in the debrief, after the player has
formed and tested an interpretation.

### Put the player inside a role

Every simulation assigns the player a credible role, remit, and limit of
authority. Avoid asking what an unspecified person “should” do from outside the
situation.

### Make every option plausible

Choices should reflect competing duties or theories of action. Avoid one
carefully written answer surrounded by negligent distractors. Inaction and
further investigation may be legitimate, but both must carry cost.

### Use evidence, not exposition

Deliver the story through artefacts: messages, incident reports, policy text,
model outputs, metrics, news items, testimony, and system changes. Each
artefact must contribute evidence, perspective, or pressure.

### Show delayed and distributed effects

An intervention may help one stakeholder while harming another, or improve an
immediate metric while weakening long-term agency. Consequences should expose
these transfers without pretending to simulate the world precisely.

### Debrief transparently

After play, reveal the signals, state changes, consequence rules, and concepts
that shaped the route. Invite disagreement rather than claiming that the model
is morally authoritative.

## Core interaction model

Each simulation contains five phases:

1. **Brief** — establish the player's role, objective, authority, and apparent
   state of the system.
2. **Investigate** — inspect evidence and decide which signals need attention.
3. **Intervene** — choose constrained actions with stated operational costs but
   uncertain effects.
4. **Escalate** — introduce new evidence shaped by current state and prior
   action.
5. **Debrief** — reconstruct the incident, compare effects, introduce concepts,
   and support reflection or replay.

A vertical-slice simulation should use two decision rounds. This creates
feedback and delayed consequences without requiring a large narrative tree.

## Consequence model

The simulation may track six internal dimensions:

- **Welfare** — physical, emotional, social, or economic effects on people
- **Agency** — ability to understand, choose, contest, or leave
- **Fairness** — distribution of benefits, burdens, errors, and remedies
- **Trust** — warranted confidence in people, systems, evidence, or institutions
- **Resilience** — capacity to detect, interrupt, recover, and learn
- **Power** — concentration, reach, and reversibility of system authority

These are not points, virtues, or a final score. During play, communicate
consequences through events and stakeholders. Use the dimensions in the debrief
to make the authored model inspectable.

## Visual direction

The interface should feel like a calm editorial incident dossier: serious,
human, and precise without resembling military command software or a generic
SaaS dashboard.

### Desired qualities

- restrained rather than theatrical;
- warm enough to support human stories;
- information-dense only where evidence requires it;
- typographically led, with illustration used selectively;
- contemporary without chasing interface trends; and
- credible in academic, portfolio, and classroom contexts.

### Proposed visual language

- A light parchment or warm neutral reading surface with high-contrast ink,
  paired with a darker incident workspace when focus is required.
- One restrained accent colour for active evidence and one caution colour for
  unresolved risk; never a rainbow of category colours.
- A literary serif for titles and selected narrative moments, paired with a
  highly legible sans serif for controls and evidence.
- Fine rules, marginal notes, timestamps, source labels, and subtle archival
  references—used as structure, not decoration.
- Minimal motion that communicates state change, chronology, or consequence.

The name “Hikayat” should lead the identity. Avoid robot heads, circuit-brain
imagery, neon gradients, surveillance clichés, and trophy aesthetics.

## Accessibility and care

- Meet WCAG 2.2 AA contrast, keyboard, focus, and semantic requirements.
- Never encode evidence or consequence solely through colour, animation, or
  sound.
- Respect reduced-motion and user font-size preferences.
- Provide content notes for grief, mental health, discrimination, violence,
  authoritarianism, and other sensitive material.
- Allow pause, exit, restart, and review without penalty.
- Do not use manipulative countdowns; represent pressure through turns or
  narrative deadlines instead.
- Store no sensitive player reflection by default.

## Definition of “game”

Hikayat qualifies as a game through state, constrained action, feedback,
consequence, and replay—not through points, competition, badges, or decorative
game language. The player is invited to understand a system, not defeat it.
